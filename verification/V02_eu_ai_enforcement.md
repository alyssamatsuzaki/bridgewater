## Item 1 — EU Digital Omnibus status (July 2026) and AI Act workplace-AI timeline

**Status: CONFIRMED** (reviewer's Dec-2027 correction is right; draft passage's "2 Aug 2026" reliance is outdated/CONTRADICTED by current law)

**What sources say:**

- The European Commission published the "Digital Omnibus on AI" proposal on **19 November 2025**, part of a broader two-regulation "Digital Omnibus" package (the other regulation amending GDPR, ePrivacy Directive, NIS2, and the Data Act). — Cooley (2025-11-24), DLA Piper GENIE, Morrison Foerster (mofo.com/resources/insights/251201-eu-digital-omnibus)
- Council and Parliament negotiators reached **provisional political agreement on 7 May 2026** (per Consilium press release headline text relayed via search); European Parliament gave formal endorsement **16 June 2026**; the Council of the EU gave final approval **29 June 2026**. — Consilium press release (URL identified, content relayed via WebSearch synthesis: https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/05/07/artificial-intelligence-council-and-parliament-agree-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/); corroborated by search-result summaries citing the same sequence.
- The text was formally adopted by Parliament and Council on **8 July 2026**, published in the EU Official Journal on **24 July 2026** as **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**, and **entered into force 27 July 2026** (three days after publication — standard EU practice). — EUR-Lex record identified at https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng (title per search snippet: "Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 8 July 2026 amends Regulations (EU) 2024/1689, (EU) 2018/1139 and (EU) 2023/1230..."); also Hunton ("EU Digital Omnibus on AI Enters Into Force"), EU Law Live, NicFab Blog, TechTimes (2026-07-27), Orrick (2026-07).
- **Key timeline change — CONFIRMED**: Regulation 2026/1744 **defers the AI Act's Annex III (stand-alone, "use-based") high-risk AI system compliance obligations from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027** — a 16-month deferral. It separately defers Annex I (product-embedded, e.g., medical devices, lifts, machinery) high-risk obligations from 2 August 2027 to 2 August 2028. Multiple independent legal-press sources converge on the same 2 Dec 2027 date: Gibson Dunn ("EU AI Act Omnibus Agreement — Postponed High-Risk Deadlines"), aiactblog.nl ("Digital Omnibus and the postponement of high-risk obligations to December 2027"), verifywise.ai, Winston Taylor, White & Case, Legalnodes, Cloud Security Alliance research note, Trilateral Research, Latham & Watkins ("AI Act Update: EU Resolves to Change Rules and Extend Deadlines"), Legiscope.
- **Substantively important for this manuscript**: Annex III (the deferred category) is the part of the AI Act that covers **employment-related AI systems** — recruitment, task allocation, and monitoring/evaluation of worker performance and behavior are the standard Annex III "employment, workers' management" high-risk use case in the Act as originally drafted (this is background knowledge about the Act's structure, not itself re-verified against primary text in this session because EUR-Lex could not be fetched — flagged as **not independently re-read**, but consistent with every secondary source's framing that the deferral affects "systems used in employment and education," per TechTimes' summary of the omnibus).
- **What still applies as of 2 August 2026, per current (post-Omnibus) law:**
  - **Article 50 transparency obligations** (labelling of AI-generated/deepfake content, disclosure that a user is interacting with an AI system) are reported as **unaffected by the Omnibus** and remain applicable from 2 August 2026 (Gibson Dunn's summary explicitly states "Article 50 transparency rules ... remain on their original timeline").
  - Prohibited AI practices (Article 5) have applied since **2 February 2025** (unaffected — Legalnodes/Legiscope), and prohibited-practice penalties (up to €35M/7% global turnover) already bite.
  - GPAI (general-purpose AI model) provider obligations have applied since **2 August 2025** (unaffected, governance chapter).
  - The Omnibus adds **two new Article 5 prohibitions** (non-consensual "nudification"/deepfake intimate imagery, and AI-generated CSAM), newly applicable from **2 December 2026**.
  - **What does NOT apply from 2 Aug 2026 anymore**: the Annex III high-risk compliance regime (risk management, technical documentation, data governance, human oversight, conformity assessment) — this is precisely the regime a workplace employee-monitoring/HR AI system would need to comply with, and it is now deferred to **2 December 2027**.

**Bottom line for the manuscript**: The reviewer is correct — December 2027 (specifically 2 December 2027) is the reported new compliance date for high-risk Annex III obligations, which is what would govern workplace AI monitoring/HR systems. The draft passage's reliance on the AI Act's high-risk regime applying to workplace AI "from 2 August 2026" is **outdated under current law** (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026). What legitimately still triggers on 2 August 2026 is the lighter-touch Article 50 transparency/labelling duty, not the full high-risk compliance package.

## Item 2 — Enforcement base rate, 2018–July 2026

### 2(a) — Formal proceedings against Meta/Microsoft/Alphabet/Amazon/OpenAI/Anthropic concerning EMPLOYEE monitoring/surveillance data

**Status: PARTLY SUPPORTED** — one confirmed case (Amazon), no evidence found for the other five.

**CNIL v. Amazon France Logistique — CONFIRMED, details corrected**

- **Authority**: CNIL (Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés), France — restricted committee ("formation restreinte"), a genuine formal sanction procedure.
- **Firm**: Amazon France Logistique (Amazon warehouse subsidiary).
- **Investigation origin**: CNIL on-site inspections in **November 2019** at Amazon's administrative offices and two warehouses, triggered by employee complaints and press reporting.
- **Decision date**: **27 December 2023** (deliberation SAN-2023-021); publicized **23 January 2024**. (Note: your brief said "Jan 2024" for the fine — the sanction decision itself is dated 27 Dec 2023; it became public in January 2024, which is presumably the source of the "Jan 2024" framing.)
- **Fine as originally set**: **€32 million.**
- **Subject**: Excessively intrusive real-time monitoring of warehouse employees via handheld scanners — three specific indicators found illegal ("Stow Machine Gun," "idle time," "latency under 10 minutes"), disproportionate 31-day retention of individual productivity data (violating data minimization), inadequate information to temporary workers, and video-surveillance transparency/security failures.
- **Formal proceeding**: Yes — statutory CNIL sanction procedure, published decision, subject to judicial appeal.
- **Outcome — IMPORTANT UPDATE not in your brief**: Amazon appealed to France's highest administrative court, the **Conseil d'État**. In **Decision No. 492830, dated 23 December 2025**, the Conseil d'État **partially overturned** the CNIL: it held the CNIL erred in finding the three performance indicators lacked a legal basis (ruling that this monitoring did not exceed what was necessary for Amazon's legitimate interests and did not disproportionately harm employee rights), while **upholding** the data-minimization violation (31-day retention). The court **reduced the fine from €32 million to €15 million**.
- **AI-training nexus**: **None found.** Nothing in the CNIL decision or the Conseil d'État ruling, per any source reviewed, frames the scanner/productivity data as being used to train an AI/ML model — it is characterized throughout as algorithmic workplace surveillance/productivity tracking, not AI-training data use.

Sources: Mondaq (2024), BCLP client alert, Ashurst client alert, Fieldfisher, ppc.land ("France slashes Amazon's GDPR fine from €32M to €15M over warehouse monitoring"), Conseil d'État decision page https://www.conseil-etat.fr/fr/arianeweb/CE/decision/2025-12-23/492830, Légifrance https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/ceta/id/CETATEXT000053163189, usine-digitale.fr, veille.portail-rgpd.com, iredic.fr (2024-01-31 note and 2026-01 jurisprudence note PDF), Privacy Laws & Business International Report #188.

### 2(b) — Formal proceedings against any large company on employee-monitoring data (not limited to the six)

**Status: CONFIRMED**

**Hamburg DPA v. H&M**

- **Authority**: Hamburg Commissioner for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (HmbBfDI), Germany.
- **Firm**: Hennes & Mauritz (H&M) — at its German service center (widely reported as the Nuremberg service center; this specific city was not stated in the retrieved search snippets, so flagged as not independently confirmed here even though it is the commonly cited detail).

- **AI-training nexus**: None — pure employee-surveillance/record-keeping case, no AI/ML training use identified in any source.

**Garante (Italy) v. OpenAI — CONFIRMED, with a major update not in your brief**

- **Authority**: Garante per la protezione dei dati personali (Italy).
- **Investigation origin**: Opened **March 2023**, following the Garante's temporary ChatGPT ban in Italy that same month.
- **Decision**: Provvedimento n. 755, dated **2 November 2024**; made public via press release **20 December 2024**.
- **Fine**: **€15 million**, plus an accessory sanction requiring OpenAI to run a **six-month institutional public-awareness campaign** across major Italian media on how ChatGPT processes personal data.
- **Subject — training-data nexus confirmed**: The Garante found OpenAI processed personal data of both users and non-users **to develop and train ChatGPT (3.5/4) without an adequate legal basis**, violated GDPR transparency/information obligations, lacked adequate age-verification, and failed to notify a March 2023 data breach within 72 hours.
- **Formal proceeding**: Yes — formal administrative sanction after a multi-year statutory investigation.
- **CRITICAL UPDATE — your brief did not have this**: OpenAI appealed to the **Tribunale di Roma**. The court first granted a **conditional suspension** of the fine (order dated **21 March 2025**, conditioned on a full-amount bank guarantee). Then, in a ruling dated **18 March 2026** (reported/published around 20–27 March 2026), the Tribunale di Roma **annulled** Provvedimento n. 755 in its entirety — **not on the merits**, but on a jurisdictional/one-stop-shop ground: OpenAI had established a single EU establishment in Ireland before the Garante's November 2024 decision, so under GDPR Articles 60–61 competence should have passed to the Irish DPC as lead authority via the cooperation mechanism. The court did not reach the substantive legal-basis/training-data questions. Sources note the Garante could still appeal to the Court of Cassation; **no confirmed appeal filing was found in this session (COULD NOT VERIFY)**.
- **Net status as of 2026-07-31**: A real formal AI-training-data enforcement action was opened, investigated, and sanctioned against OpenAI — but the sanction has since been **judicially vacated on jurisdictional grounds**, and the substantive question was never resolved by any court. A secondary source (Cross-Border Data Forum) explicitly frames the broader landscape as "**A Lot of Noise, One Fine, Zero Survivors**" — i.e., of GDPR enforcement actions specifically targeting generative-AI training in Europe through mid-2026, this Garante/OpenAI case was the only one that reached a final regulatory sanction, and even that one did not survive judicial review.

## Item 4 — Legal characterizations: Italy Workers' Statute Art. 4 and German keystroke-logging case law

**Status: CONFIRMED (both)**

**Italy — Workers' Statute Article 4 (Law 300/1970, as amended by Legislative Decree 151/2015 "Jobs Act")**

- Remote/technological monitoring tools ("dedicated" equipment, e.g., software that could monitor employee activity) may be installed **only for organizational and production needs, workplace safety, and protection of company assets** — not for indiscriminate surveillance of employee activity.
- Deployment requires either **(a)** a prior collective agreement with internal trade union representatives (first RSU, then RSA, escalating to the most representative unions at territorial/national level if no local representation exists or agreement is not reached), **or**, absent agreement, **(b)** authorization from the competent **territorial Labour Inspectorate** (Ispettorato Territoriale del Lavoro).
- Employees must be adequately informed about any monitoring tools and the related data-processing rules.
- This matches the manuscript's characterization ("monitoring only for narrow purposes with union/labor-inspectorate authorization").

Sources: Lexology ("Remote controls on employees under Italian law"), Rivermate Italy remote-work guide, Global Law Experts, De Luca & Partners, Willkie Compliance Concourse, securindex.com (PDF text of Art. 4), employee-monitoring.net.

**Germany — Bundesarbeitsgericht (Federal Labour Court) keystroke-logging ruling**

- **Case**: BAG, judgment of **27 July 2017**, docket **2 AZR 681/16**.
- **Holding**: The employer's **covert use of keylogger software** to monitor an employee (a web developer, dismissed after the employer discovered extensive private computer use via the keylogger) was unlawful absent a **concrete, documented suspicion of a criminal offense or other serious breach of duty** by that specific employee. Because there was no such prior suspicion, the keylogger evidence violated the employee's constitutional right to informational self-determination and was **inadmissible as evidence** in the dismissal proceedings.
- This matches the manuscript's characterization ("German case law confining keystroke logging," with the year correctly identified as 2017).

Sources: Data Protection Report (dataprotectionreport.com, 2017-08), WINHELLER, Orrick Employment Law blog (2017-08-07), Bird & Bird (twobirds.com), Hogan Lovells, PLANIT//LEGAL, BleepingComputer, Lexology (two separate write-ups).
