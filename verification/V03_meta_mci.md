## 1. Does the program exist, and under what exact name? What has Meta itself publicly confirmed?

**Status: CONFIRMED-AS-COMPANY-DISCLOSURE (existence, name, no-opt-out policy, and the pause) — with a caveat on the exact pause date**

- The program exists and is consistently named **"Model Capability Initiative (MCI)"** across a wide, independent set of outlets (TechCrunch, Fortune, CNBC, Reuters-syndicated wire copy, Wired-derived coverage, Biometric Update, Computerworld, eWeek). No outlet used a different name for this specific program in my searches. (One WebSearch synthesis briefly introduced a name "Agent Transformation Accelerator (ATA)" in connection with the mouse-tracking protest; a follow-up search showed "Agent Transformation Accelerator" is actually a *different*, unrelated Meta internal AI-adoption program ("AI for Work"), not MCI. Flagging this as a synthesis-tool conflation I caught and rejected — MCI is the correct/only name for the tracking program in question.)
- **Company confirmation of existence/mechanics:** Reported via an internal Meta memo (from the Meta Superintelligence Labs team, per TechCrunch/Fortune-sourced coverage, April 2026) describing the tool as capturing mouse movements, click locations, keystrokes, and periodic screen content on a pre-approved list of work apps (Gmail, Google Chat, Metamate, VS Code, etc.), limited to US-based full-time employees and contingent workers on company-owned devices (not phones).
- **Company confirmation of no broad opt-out:** Meta CTO **Andrew Bosworth** is quoted directly (reported by multiple outlets covering the April 2026 rollout): *"There is no option to opt out of this on your work provided laptop."* This is a company-official (CTO) statement, relayed through press — i.e., the underlying fact ("no opt-out") is company-disclosed, not press-invented. Meta later added a partial concession — the ability to pause data collection in 30-minute increments and to request an exemption — but multiple outlets state Meta did **not** reverse the no-broad-opt-out policy.
- **Company confirmation of the pause:** A Meta spokesperson, named in search results as **Tracy Clayton**, is quoted: *"We have carefully designed this program with privacy safeguards and while we have no indication at this time that any data was improperly accessed by Meta employees, we're pausing it while we investigate."* This is a direct company statement confirming the pause. Separately, CTO Bosworth is reported (via a later interview, apparently with The Atlantic, as relayed by syndicated tech coverage) describing the underlying incident as a researcher moving "transformed employee data" to "a place it wasn't supposed to go" and characterizing it as not a breach and with no sign of foul play.
- **Pause date — some inconsistency across outlets:** Search results show the pause reported as **"Monday, June 22, 2026"** in Reuters-derived wire copy (syndicated via Yahoo Finance Canada, GVWire, MyJoyOnline — all dated 2026-06-22), but other outlets (gHacks, tech.yahoo.com, and one AI-synthesized summary) date the public confirmation to **June 23, 2026**. This is consistent with an announcement made June 22 (US Pacific time) landing as June 23 in some republished/international copy, but I could not verify the exact original wire timestamp because I could not open the Reuters article directly (fetch blocked). **The manuscript's "June 23" date is plausible and within the range found in reporting, but I could not pin it to a single authoritative timestamp.**

**Sourcing chain:** TechCrunch (2026-04-21, "Meta will record employees' keystrokes and use it to train its AI models"), Fortune (2026-04-21, "Meta will start tracking employees' screens and keystrokes to train AI tools"), CNBC (2026-04-22), Reuters wire copy syndicated via GVWire (2026-06-22, "Meta to Pause Internal Mouse-Tracking Tech While Examining Data Security Issues") and Yahoo Finance Canada (2026-06-22), gHacks (2026-06-23, "Meta Pauses Employee Mouse-Tracking AI Training Program After Internal Data Exposure").
**Channel:** WebSearch synthesis (WebFetch blocked/403 on all of these domains). Access date: 2026-07-31.

## 2. The 45,000-internal-tables figure

**Status: CONFIRMED-AS-PRESS-REPORTING (not company disclosure) — sourced to Wired, citing an internal Meta security notice**

- The figure traces to **Wired**, described across several secondary write-ups as reporting that *"according to documents reviewed by Wired, a security notice said that 'employee data across 45,000 hive tables' were exposed."* "Hive tables" = Apache Hive data-warehouse tables, an internal Meta data-storage format — i.e., this is Wired's characterization of an internal Meta security notice it says it obtained/reviewed, not a number Meta put out in a public statement.
- Multiple secondary outlets (MLQ News, IBTimes UK, Digital Trends, Fast Company, TipRanks/"the Fly," privacyscrubber.com) all attribute the 45,000-table figure back to Wired's reporting, not to a Meta press release or blog post. I could not open the original Wired article directly (wired.com fetch was outright blocked by the tool) to confirm exact headline, byline, or publish date, so I cannot give you the canonical Wired URL — only the secondary sourcing chain that consistently names Wired as the origin.
- **Meta's response to this specific figure:** I found a general Meta spokesperson statement responding to "requests for comment" about the exposure story (the Tracy Clayton quote in §1: safeguards language + confirmation of the pause + "no indication... data was improperly accessed"). I did **not** find any instance of Meta specifically confirming, disputing, or giving an alternate number for "45,000 tables" — the spokesperson statement is a general non-denial about the program and pause, not a number-level confirmation. Meta neither disputed the figure nor put its own number forward in what I could find.
- This matches the reviewer's concern: **the 45,000-table figure is press-reported (originating with Wired, based on a document Wired says it reviewed), not a number Meta itself has publicly stated.**

**Sourcing chain:** Wired (original; exact date/byline/URL not independently confirmed — could not fetch wired.com), relayed by MLQ News, IBTimes UK ("Meta's Creepy Model Capability Program Unmasked as Security Glitch Exposes 1000s of Workers' Private Messages"), Fast Company (2026-06-24 window), TipRanks/"the Fly" ("Meta internally exposed data from employee-tracking program, Wired reports"), privacyscrubber.com ("Meta's Internal AI Initiative Leaks 45,000 Database Tables").

## 3. The 1,600-employee petition figure

**Status: CONFIRMED-AS-PRESS-REPORTING (not company disclosure)**

- Multiple outlets report **"more than 1,600" Meta employees** signed an internal petition opposing MCI, and — importantly — this petition **predates** the June security-exposure incident. One search synthesis specifically cited the count as **"1,600 signatures as of June 3"** (2026), i.e., roughly three weeks before the June pause, which fits the manuscript's framing of the petition as a separate, earlier data point layered onto the later "pause" story.
- The petition appears to originate from employee-organized protest activity reported around May 2026 (flyers posted in Meta offices directing staff to an online petition), which multiple outlets (Engadget, a Reuters "Exclusive," Yahoo News, TheNextWeb, IBTimes) covered as breaking/scoop reporting — i.e., **press reporting on an internal/employee-run petition**, not a number Meta itself released.
- **Note on figure inconsistency:** at least one outlet (Technobezz) reports the number as **"more than 1,500"** workers rather than 1,600, suggesting the count may have been a moving target reported at different snapshots by different outlets, or a minor discrepancy between outlets. This is a soft inconsistency worth flagging to the author — "1,600" is the more commonly repeated figure, but it is not universal across outlets.
- I found no Meta statement confirming or disputing the petition signature count. Meta's public response (per multiple outlets) was limited to product concessions (30-minute pause windows, exemption requests) rather than confirming/disputing petition numbers.

**Sourcing chain:** Reported via employee-organized petition/protest coverage, first surfaced by outlets including Engadget ("Meta employees are protesting the company's mouse tracking program") and a Reuters exclusive ("Exclusive-Meta employees launch protest against mouse-tracking tech at US offices," syndicated via Yahoo Finance), with the "1,600" figure repeated in Fortune-adjacent and Business Insider-adjacent coverage per search synthesis (I could not open Business Insider directly — domain blocked by the fetch tool — so I cannot 100% rule out BI as the originating outlet for the specific number, only that it is one of several outlets repeating it). Later repeated in the June pause coverage (MLQ News, gblock.app, Class Action U, Malwarebytes) as scene-setting context for the security incident.
**Channel:** WebSearch synthesis (WebFetch blocked on engadget.com, businessinsider.com, boingboing.net). Access date: 2026-07-31.

## 4. Characterization of the captured/exposed data (private conversations, performance records, tax filings, medical data)

**Status: SPLIT — see below. "Private conversations" and "performance data" = CONFIRMED-AS-PRESS-REPORTING (sourced to an internal document). "Tax filings" and "medical data" = COULD NOT VERIFY at the same evidentiary level.**

- The core, most-repeated data-type claim traces directly to the same Wired-sourced internal security notice as the 45,000-table figure. The notice is quoted (via secondary outlets relaying Wired) as saying the exposed data included **"full prompts and transcriptions, private conversations, people, and performance data."** This is consistently repeated across MLQ News, IBTimes UK, Digital Trends, Fast Company, and others, all attributing it to the same underlying document/Wired report. This is **press reporting of an internal Meta document's own language** — a stronger sourcing chain than a bare press paraphrase, but still not a public Meta disclosure; Meta's own public spokesperson statement (Tracy Clayton quote) does not itself enumerate data types.
- **Tax filings and medical data are a materially weaker claim.** I found this specific pairing ("tax and medical information") in only a cluster of secondary/aggregator-tier outlets (BigGo Finance, sqmagazine.co.uk, technology.org, eWeek, hr-brew.com) which appear to be repeating each other rather than each independently sourcing it. The most concrete attribution I found was BigGo Finance quoting a **single, unnamed employee**: *"I have accessed both personal tax and medical information through my work computer, as have many thousands of employees. We were told this data would be protected and only used for valid business purposes after aggressive filtering."* This is a first-person anecdotal claim relayed by a lower-tier aggregator, not something that appeared in the primary Wired-sourced security-notice language ("full prompts and transcriptions, private conversations, people, and performance data") that anchors the rest of the story. I could not find this claim corroborated in the higher-tier outlets (Reuters, TechCrunch, Fortune, CNBC) covered above, and I could not open the aggregator articles directly to check how they sourced the anonymous quote (WebFetch blocked).
- **Recommendation for the manuscript:** "private conversations" and "performance records" are reasonably well-supported as press-reported (traceable to an internal document quoted by Wired and relayed by multiple independent outlets). "Tax filings" and "medical data" rest on a much thinner, single-anecdote sourcing chain from lower-tier aggregators and should either be cut, heavily hedged ("some employees reported..."), or independently re-verified with direct access to the original Wired piece or a primary outlet before being stated as fact.

**Sourcing chain:** Wired (internal security-notice language: "full prompts and transcriptions, private conversations, people, and performance data") relayed via MLQ News, IBTimes UK, Digital Trends, Fast Company, TipRanks; tax/medical claim via BigGo Finance ("Meta Halts Employee-Tracking AI Program After Massive Data Leak Exposes Keystrokes, Private Chats, and Tax Info"), sqmagazine.co.uk, technology.org, eWeek, hr-brew.com (mutually repeating, single anonymous-employee attribution).
**Channel:** WebSearch synthesis (WebFetch blocked on all listed domains). Access date: 2026-07-31.

## 5. Is "no broad opt-out" supported, and by whom?

**Status: CONFIRMED-AS-COMPANY-DISCLOSURE**

- Best-supported claim in the set. Meta CTO Andrew Bosworth is directly quoted, at the program's April 2026 launch, saying: **"There is no option to opt out of this on your work provided laptop."** This is a Meta executive's own on-the-record statement (relayed through press coverage of the launch — TechCrunch/Fortune/CNBC-era reporting), not a press inference.
- This is corroborated by the reported employee backlash itself (petition demanding cancellation, complaints that "no completed privacy reviews were provided" when employees asked) and by Meta's own subsequent partial concession — offering 30-minute pause windows and an exemption-request process — which multiple outlets frame explicitly as *falling short of* a broad opt-out, i.e., even Meta's remedial response is reported as preserving the no-broad-opt-out default rather than reversing it.
- I did not find any Meta statement contradicting or walking back the "no opt-out" characterization.

**Sourcing chain:** TechCrunch (2026-04-21), Fortune (2026-04-21), CNBC (2026-04-22) — Bosworth quote at launch; TheNextWeb ("Meta offers 30-minute pause on employee tracking after backlash") and Entrepreneur ("Meta Is Tracking Employee Keystrokes, Clicks—Causing Backlash") — later partial-concession coverage confirming no reversal of the no-opt-out default.
